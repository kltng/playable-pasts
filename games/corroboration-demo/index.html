The Grain Shipment

Four accounts of one event disagree. Students work out which statement the evidence can carry — and meet a round where the honest answer is that it cannot be settled.

Teacher guide — The Grain Shipment

One file. Ten minutes. No accounts, no wifi needed.

What the game argues

That the reliability of a source depends on who made it, when, for whom, and what they needed the reader to believe — and that a well-run enquiry sometimes ends in "the evidence cannot settle this."

The second point is the one students resist. Round two is built so that every confident answer is wrong.

What students do

Three rounds. Each gives them two or three short documents and one question with three answers. They choose; the game explains why that answer holds or fails; they move on.

The three rounds escalate deliberately:

  1. Two records that look contradictory are not. A later chronicle inflates a number, while two contemporary records fit together once read closely.
  2. Two interested parties, and a missing disinterested record. The correct answer is that it cannot be settled.
  3. Agreement is not corroboration. Two accounts share wording, so they are one claim, not two. An internal household record beats both.

Running one session

TimeWhat happens
0–3 minBriefing. Say only this: every document is invented, the skill is real, and there will be a round with no right answer. Do not explain source criticism first — the game is the explanation.
3–10 minPlay. Individually, or in pairs on one device. Pairs are better: the argument between two students is the learning.
10–25 minDebrief. The five questions on the ending screen. Question 2 and question 4 are the ones worth real time.

If you have a full period, follow the debrief by handing out two conflicting sources from your own unit and asking students to run the same enquiry with no game at all.

Getting it to students

Post game.html to your LMS as a file, or email it. They download and double-click. It needs no internet after that, stores nothing, and collects no data about them.

If something goes wrong

Refresh the page — the game restarts cleanly and nothing is stored. If the file will not open, it was probably saved as game.html.txt; rename it. If the text looks like garbled symbols, the file was saved in the wrong encoding; download it again.

Editing it yourself

Open game.html in any plain text editor. The block marked GAME_DATA near the top holds every word in the game — documents, questions, answers, explanations, debrief. Change the text between the quotation marks, keep the commas and brackets, save, and reopen.

The most valuable edit is replacing round two's documents with a genuine dispute from your own course. The structure carries over unchanged.

History Bible — The Grain Shipment

This game has no corpus. It is a method exercise with invented documents. This file exists because every Playable Past game ships one, and because a bible that honestly records "no sources" is more useful than a fabricated one.

1. Source inventory

None. No primary source, dataset, image, or secondary work was used or consulted.

Every document shown in the game was written for it. They carry no source IDs because there is nothing to cite, and they must never be quoted as evidence of anything.

2. Why invent rather than use real sources

A demonstration needs documents whose problems are unambiguous: a number that inflates over time, two interested parties with no neutral record between them, and two accounts that look independent but share wording.

Real archives supply all three, but rarely in ten minutes of reading and never without the period knowledge students may not have yet. Using invented documents keeps the method in the foreground.

The trade-off is real and is stated in the game itself: students practise the method on material that cannot reward them for knowing the period. This is a warm-up, not a substitute for work on real sources.

4. Integrity safeguards

SafeguardHow it is handled
Synthetic-source confusionThe largest risk here. Addressed three times: a notice before play, the Sources & assumptions screen, and this file.
False precisionScoring implies source criticism has right answers. Named as an assumption (C008) and put to students in debrief question 5.
Archive biasRound 2 turns it into the lesson: the one disinterested record is missing, and that absence is the finding.
TeleologyNot applicable — no historical outcome is modelled.
PresentismDocuments avoid period-specific institutions, so no modern category is smuggled in.
Trauma as entertainmentNot applicable — a grain robbery with no casualties, chosen partly for that reason.

5. Open questions

  • Does scoring teach the wrong lesson about a practice that is argued rather than marked? Debrief question 5 raises it with students rather than resolving it.
  • Would replacing round two with a genuine dispute from the instructor's own unit be strictly better? Probably yes. The teacher guide recommends exactly that edit.

Fixed during testing

Running the keyboard pass found that focus dropped to <body> every time a screen changed, so a keyboard or screen-reader user was returned to the top of the document after every choice. Each screen now moves focus to its own heading. Row 8 was re-run after the fix.

Notes

Row 12 is an honest downgrade. An earlier draft of this ledger recorded a timed play-through that had not happened. The word count supports the ten-minute figure, but a word count is not a clock, and nobody has watched a student play this.

Rows 15 and 16 are the ones that matter to anyone adopting this game. Semantic HTML is a good sign, not a screen-reader test, and nothing here can tell you how your lecture-hall machine will behave.

Re-run rows 1–14 after any edit to game.html. Editing only the text inside GAME_DATA cannot break rows 1–5, but it can break row 12.
