Teacher guide — Many Centres, One World

A map quiz of the great voyages, c. 1400–1600, for an undergraduate world-history survey.

What the game argues

The age of the great voyages had many centres. Ming China, the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian ports, the Swahili coast, the Ottomans, the Atlantic powers and the states of the Americas were all part of it. Goods such as pepper, nutmeg, silk, porcelain and silver tied them together.

The game makes this argument by its choices. It starts in East Asia, not in Lisbon. It gives each region about five places. It ends by asking students why the usual "Age of Discovery" story starts in Europe. Students should notice these choices, and the debrief asks them to.

What students do

Each question names an event or a trade ("In 1567 the Ming made this Fujian harbour the legal port for overseas trade. Where?"). Four lettered pins appear on a world map. Students pick one by clicking the pin or the button below the map. Then they read what happened there, how sure historians are (attested, interpretation, or contested), and which sources say so.

There are five rounds and 29 questions:

  1. East Asia (5)
  2. The Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia (8)
  3. The Mediterranean, the Atlantic and Africa (6)
  4. The Americas (5)
  5. Connections (5): where did silver, silk, pepper and nutmeg mostly go?

A button switches the map between an Atlantic-centred and a Pacific-centred view. The last screen shows every place on one map, a score by region, a closing question, and the debrief questions.

The question about Elmina Castle is not scored. It became a centre of the Atlantic slave trade, and finding a place of enslavement should not earn a point.

Running one session (about 25 minutes)

  • Briefing, 3 minutes. "This is a quiz, but it is also an argument. Watch which places it picks and in what order."
  • Play, about 15 minutes. Students play alone on their own laptops. There is no time limit. Nobody has timed a real play-through yet, so time it yourself once.
  • Debrief, 7–10 minutes. Use the questions below. Ask students to switch the map view before question 2.

Debrief questions

These are on the game's last screen too.

  1. This quiz chose the places, the rounds and the order. Which centre did it treat as most important? What does putting the Atlantic round third, not first, argue?
  2. Switch the map between the Atlantic and the Pacific. Which view made Europe look central? Which made the Pacific look like a highway instead of an edge?
  3. The quiz asks you to find places, not people. Whose experience of these voyages is missing from a map of ports and routes?
  4. Pick one place marked "contested" in the Sources screen. What would change in the story if the other reading were right?
  5. The game gives a point for the right pin. What does that reward, and what kind of historical thinking does it not reward?

The closing screen also asks each student to write one sentence: why might the story so often be told from Europe? You can collect these sentences, or ask students to screenshot the final screen. The game sends nothing anywhere.

Before class

  • Check the answers. The agent found the sources itself, and no historian has checked them yet. Read the History Bible, especially the eight items that rest on Wikipedia alone, and change anything you disagree with.
  • Open the file once on the classroom setup, and through your LMS if students will get it there.
  • Time one play-through.

If something goes wrong

  • The page looks broken or blank: make sure the file was saved as .html, then open it again in Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.
  • A student wants to start again: the Start over button at the top clears their answers.
  • A student refreshed the page: the game remembers where they were in that browser.
  • Saving does not work (for example in a private window): the game still plays; it just cannot continue after a refresh.

Changing the game

All the text is in one block near the top of game.html, marked GAME CONTENT. You can edit it in any plain-text editor (TextEdit in plain-text mode, Notepad, VS Code). Each place has a question, an explanation, a map position, how sure it is, and source numbers. To remove a question, delete its { ... } block and the comma after it. Save and reopen the file to check. If you change a fact, change the History Bible too.

History Bible — Many Centres, One World

A map quiz of the great voyages, c. 1400–1600, for an undergraduate world-history survey.

What the game argues

The age of the great voyages had many centres, not one. Ming China, the ports of the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia, the Swahili coast, the Ottomans, the Atlantic powers and the states of the Americas were all part of it, and goods such as pepper, cloves, nutmeg, silk, porcelain and silver tied them together. The game makes this argument through its choice of places, the order of the rounds (East Asia first, the Atlantic third), and a closing question that asks why the usual story starts in Europe.

How this was made

  • Instructor's material: none. The instructor asked the agent to find the sources.
  • Research: an AI agent (Claude) searched for and read every source below on 13 October 2026, and wrote each question and explanation from them. It did not use its own memory for facts. Each item lists the sources it rests on.
  • Spot check: a second pass by the same agent re-read five facts in the saved source pages (Iwami 1526, Pizarro's 180 men, da Gama leaving Lisbon on 8 July 1497, the Casa de Contratación in Seville in 1503, the giraffe from Malindi). All five matched.
  • Not done: no historian has checked the items against the sources. See the test ledger.

Weak points to know about

  • Britannica and UNESCO block automatic downloads, so their pages were read through saved copies in the Internet Archive. Many Britannica pages could not be read at all.
  • These items rest on Wikipedia alone: C02, C11, C12, C13, C15, C20, X02, X04. Each Wikipedia link is one fixed version of the article.
  • The Ming History (明史) biography of Zheng He was read in the Kanripo digital edition (Siku Quanshu, Wenyuange). That edition numbers it juan 491; standard printed editions call it juan 304.
  • Places are shown at their modern locations on today's coastlines (Natural Earth, public domain, 1:50m land outlines, version 5.1.2).

Claim types

  • Attested: the event, place and date are stated in a primary source or agreed in standard reference works.
  • Interpretation: historians broadly agree, but the claim is a judgement drawn from evidence.
  • Contested: historians disagree; the explanation says how.

Nothing in the game is invented: no characters, documents or events are made up. The modelling choices (which places, which rounds, one main destination per trade connection, the wrong answers being other places from the quiz) are listed on the game's Sources & assumptions screen.

Safeguards

  • Elmina Castle (C18) became a centre of the Atlantic slave trade. The question is not scored and shows no "right" or "wrong" mark: finding a place of enslavement should not earn a point.
  • Potosí (C23) names the mita forced-labour draft in its explanation.
  • Tenochtitlan (C21) names the Tlaxcalan allies and smallpox, so the fall is not told as a story of a few Spaniards alone.
  • No percentage of world silver is given anywhere, because the sources disagree (60% or 80% for Potosí; one third for Japan).

Items dropped or changed, and why

  • Cambay / Gujarat: dropped to keep the list at 24. The Gujarati link survives in the Malindi item (da Gama's pilot was probably Gujarati). Wikipedia 'Khambhat' (Duarte Barbosa on Cambay cloth going to Mogadishu) would support a Cambay item if you want one later.
  • Aden: not used as a map item. Wikipedia 'Aden' has the 1513 Portuguese siege and the 1538 Ottoman takeover, if needed.
  • Venice: not a map item. It appears in the pepper connection (X04).
  • Cuzco: not used; Cajamarca was chosen for the Inca item because the capture of Atahuallpa happened there.
  • Percentages of world silver (Potosí '60%' or '80%', Japan 'one third', China 'most of the world's silver') are left out. The sources disagree or call them estimates.
  • Zheng He's ship numbers and ship sizes are left out of the game text. Sources disagree (62 ships in the Mingshi and Britannica; 317 ships in Columbia's page).

Contested points flagged in claims.json

  • C10 Malindi: the year of the Chinese visit and the giraffe come from Wikipedia only; the identity of da Gama's pilot (not Ibn Majid; probably Gujarati).
  • C20 Guanahani: which island Columbus reached is debated.
  • X01 Silver to China: how much of the world's silver ended up in China is debated.
  • Smaller date differences noted: Macau 1553/1554/1557; Dias 'March' or 'May' 1488 at the Cape; Atahuallpa executed 26 July (Wikipedia) or 29 August (Britannica) 1533; Potosí silver found 1545 (UNESCO's Dutch summary says 1542–1545).

Instructor's five-minute test

  1. Download game.html, turn off the wifi, and double-click it. Answer three questions.
  2. Press Start over, then play one question with only Tab and Enter.
  3. Press Sources & assumptions and read the entry for one place you know well.
  4. Open it once through your LMS on the classroom computer.
