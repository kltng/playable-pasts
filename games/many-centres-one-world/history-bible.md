# History Bible — Many Centres, One World

A map quiz of the great voyages, c. 1400–1600, for an undergraduate world-history survey.

## What the game argues

The age of the great voyages had many centres, not one. Ming China, the ports of the Indian
Ocean and Southeast Asia, the Swahili coast, the Ottomans, the Atlantic powers and the states
of the Americas were all part of it, and goods such as pepper, cloves, nutmeg, silk, porcelain
and silver tied them together. The game makes this argument through its choice of places, the
order of the rounds (East Asia first, the Atlantic third), and a closing question that asks why
the usual story starts in Europe.

## How this was made

- **Instructor's material:** none. The instructor asked the agent to find the sources.
- **Research:** an AI agent (Claude) searched for and read every source below on 13 October
  2026, and wrote each question and explanation from them. It did not use its own memory for
  facts. Each item lists the sources it rests on.
- **Spot check:** a second pass by the same agent re-read five facts in the saved source pages
  (Iwami 1526, Pizarro's 180 men, da Gama leaving Lisbon on 8 July 1497, the Casa de
  Contratación in Seville in 1503, the giraffe from Malindi). All five matched.
- **Not done:** no historian has checked the items against the sources. See the test ledger.

## Weak points to know about

- Britannica and UNESCO block automatic downloads, so their pages were read through saved
  copies in the Internet Archive. Many Britannica pages could not be read at all.
- These items rest on **Wikipedia alone**: C02, C11, C12, C13, C15, C20, X02, X04. Each Wikipedia link is one
  fixed version of the article.
- The Ming History (明史) biography of Zheng He was read in the Kanripo digital edition (Siku
  Quanshu, Wenyuange). That edition numbers it juan 491; standard printed editions call it
  juan 304.
- Places are shown at their modern locations on today's coastlines (Natural Earth, public
  domain, 1:50m land outlines, version 5.1.2).

## Claim types

- **Attested:** the event, place and date are stated in a primary source or agreed in standard
  reference works.
- **Interpretation:** historians broadly agree, but the claim is a judgement drawn from evidence.
- **Contested:** historians disagree; the explanation says how.

Nothing in the game is invented: no characters, documents or events are made up. The modelling
choices (which places, which rounds, one main destination per trade connection, the wrong
answers being other places from the quiz) are listed on the game's *Sources & assumptions*
screen.

## Safeguards

- **Elmina Castle (C18)** became a centre of the Atlantic slave trade. The question is not
  scored and shows no "right" or "wrong" mark: finding a place of enslavement should not earn a
  point.
- **Potosí (C23)** names the mita forced-labour draft in its explanation.
- **Tenochtitlan (C21)** names the Tlaxcalan allies and smallpox, so the fall is not told as a
  story of a few Spaniards alone.
- No percentage of world silver is given anywhere, because the sources disagree (60% or 80% for
  Potosí; one third for Japan).

- Britannica and UNESCO block automatic downloads (a Cloudflare check). So I read their pages through saved copies in the Internet Archive Wayback Machine. The URL given is the exact saved copy I read. The date of each saved copy is in the title.

### Items dropped or changed, and why

- Cambay / Gujarat: dropped to keep the list at 24. The Gujarati link survives in the Malindi item (da Gama's pilot was probably Gujarati). Wikipedia 'Khambhat' (Duarte Barbosa on Cambay cloth going to Mogadishu) would support a Cambay item if you want one later.
- Aden: not used as a map item. Wikipedia 'Aden' has the 1513 Portuguese siege and the 1538 Ottoman takeover, if needed.
- Venice: not a map item. It appears in the pepper connection (X04).
- Cuzco: not used; Cajamarca was chosen for the Inca item because the capture of Atahuallpa happened there.
- Percentages of world silver (Potosí '60%' or '80%', Japan 'one third', China 'most of the world's silver') are left out. The sources disagree or call them estimates.
- Zheng He's ship numbers and ship sizes are left out of the game text. Sources disagree (62 ships in the Mingshi and Britannica; 317 ships in Columbia's page).

### Contested points flagged in claims.json

- C10 Malindi: the year of the Chinese visit and the giraffe come from Wikipedia only; the identity of da Gama's pilot (not Ibn Majid; probably Gujarati).
- C20 Guanahani: which island Columbus reached is debated.
- X01 Silver to China: how much of the world's silver ended up in China is debated.
- Smaller date differences noted: Macau 1553/1554/1557; Dias 'March' or 'May' 1488 at the Cape; Atahuallpa executed 26 July (Wikipedia) or 29 August (Britannica) 1533; Potosí silver found 1545 (UNESCO's Dutch summary says 1542–1545).
